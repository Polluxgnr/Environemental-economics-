## Slide 4: Method (2:15–3:20)

### Skeleton to memorise

Learn this chain of keywords, not the sentences. The sentences come back once you know the chain.

> **France** → **seasons = 92 %** (mild January vs cool July) → **same month, 1961–1990** (30-year normal, WMO) → **anomaly** → **Δ = +1.74** (last 10 years vs reference) → **σ = 0.51** (ordinary year vs trend) → **3.4 > 2** (could the decade pass for an ordinary year? no) → **rain: same steps, in %**

### The text

> Here is the whole method on one country, France. On the left, raw monthly temperature. Almost everything you see is the seasons: they are 92 percent of the variation, so a mild January still looks colder than a cool July. To remove them, we compare each month with the same month over 1961 to 1990: a thirty-year climate normal, and the WMO's reference period. What is left is the anomaly. On the right, the yearly anomaly, summed up by two numbers. The change, delta: the last ten years against the reference, +1.74 degrees. The noise, sigma: how far an ordinary year strays from the trend, 0.51. Their ratio, 3.4, asks one question: could the last decade pass for an ordinary year? Above two, it could not. Rainfall goes through exactly the same steps, against the same reference, in percent of normal.

**What to point at:**
- the zig-zag on the left ("almost everything you see");
- the shaded block 1961–1990 on the right ("reference");
- the black arrow ("delta");
- the grey band ("sigma").

### The main points, so you can say them in your own words

**Why remove the seasons?** France's temperature swings by about 15 °C between January (3.0 °C on average in 1961–1990) and July (18.6 °C). That seasonal cycle is 92 % of all the variation, so a warming of 1–2 °C is invisible in the raw series. Subtracting each month's own normal leaves only "how unusual was this month for its time of year". That is the **anomaly**.

**Why 1961–1990?**
1. Climate is defined as 30-year averages. 1961–1990 is the WMO's long-standing reference "normal".
2. It comes before most of the warming, so it is a clean "old climate".
3. It is the first 30 years for which we also have World Bank growth data.

The Atlas gives absolute values (°C, mm/day), not anomalies. So choosing the reference is our decision, and we say so.

**What is Δ (delta)?** The average anomaly over the last ten years, 2016–2025. Since the reference period averages to zero by construction, Δ is "how much warmer the recent decade is than the old normal". We use ten years, not just 2025, so that one odd year cannot drive the result.

**What is σ (sigma)?**
- First, draw the straight trend line through 1961–2025.
- Each year sits a bit above or below that line. σ is the typical size of those gaps (their standard deviation).
- In words: σ is how much one ordinary year wobbles.
- We measure the wobble around the *trend*, not around a flat line. Otherwise the warming itself would count as "noise" and make σ look bigger than it is.

**Why "above two, it could not"?** Year-to-year wobbles are roughly bell-shaped. About 95 % of ordinary years stay within 2σ of where they are expected to be. So if the old climate were still in place, a year more than 2σ above the 1961–1990 normal would be rare (about 1 in 40 on the warm side). France's *ten-year average* sits 3.4σ above it. That is not a run of bad luck: the normal itself has moved.

**The blank columns in the right-hand chart.** You may notice gaps in the bars around 1991, 1996 and 2010. These are not missing data. Those years were very slightly *below* the 1961–1990 normal (−0.02, −0.08 and −0.07 °C), so they show as tiny blue bars you can barely see. Every month from 1940 to 2025 is in the data for all twelve countries.

## Slide 5: The full record (3:20–4:05)

### Skeleton

> **12 rows, 1 cell = 1 year, since 1940** → **same reference, marked on top** → **temperature: blue → red after the 1980s; every country-year since 2016 warmer** → **rainfall: brown dry / blue wet, flickers, no colour trend** → **before 1961 faded: fewer observations** → *"Is the contrast as clear in numbers?"*

### The text

> Now all twelve countries, every year since 1940: one row per country, one cell per year, and both panels measured against the same reference, marked on top. On the left, temperature: mostly blue before the 1980s, red after, and every country-year since 2016 is warmer than its 1961–1990 normal. On the right, rainfall: brown is a drier year, blue a wetter one. It flickers, with no colour trend. The years before 1961 are faded: fewer observations stand behind them, so we show them but measure change from 1961. Is the contrast as clear in numbers?

**What to point at:**
- the black bar "reference 1961–1990" above each panel;
- the dark-red right edge of the temperature panel;
- the brown and blue rainfall panel.

### The main points

**Every country-year since 2016 is warmer.** That is 12 countries × 10 years = 120 cells, and all 120 are above zero. The coolest of them is Australia 2022, still +0.07 °C.

**Rainfall is in percent, not millimetres.** Ten millimetres is a lot in Senegal (590 mm a year) and almost nothing in Vietnam (2,070 mm). So each year's rain is expressed as a percentage above or below *that country's own* 1961–1990 average. +20 % means 20 % more rain than the old normal, wherever you are.

**Why faded before 1961?** ERA5 is a reanalysis: a weather model continuously corrected with real observations (stations, balloons, and satellites from 1979). Before about 1950 there were far fewer observations, especially for rainfall. So we show those years for context, but every number we quote starts in 1961.

**The white lines between rows** separate the continents (Europe, Africa, Asia-Pacific, Americas). They are not gaps in the data.

## Slide 6: Signal versus noise (4:05–5:10)

### Skeleton

> **It is.** → **dot = change, grey = 1σ and 2σ** → **temperature 11/12 beyond 2σ** → **Mexico +1.4 = 5.2σ** (barely varies) → **Germany warmed more, 2.9σ** (winters swing) → **Australia exception 1.7** → **rain: smaller than one year's swing everywhere** → **but a 10-year average is precise: Brazil, India, Kenya, Senegal measurable; Kenya −14 %** → **does not follow warming** → **temperature moved, rainfall much less** → *"But when in the year does the warming happen?"*

### The text

> It is. Each dot is a country's change; the grey bands are one and two sigma of its ordinary variation. Temperature is beyond two sigma in 11 of 12 countries. Mexico is the clearest case: +1.4 degrees is 5.2 sigma, because its temperature barely varies. Germany warmed more, but its winters swing so much that it is 2.9 sigma. Australia is the one exception, at 1.7. Rainfall is different: everywhere, the change is smaller than one ordinary year's swing, so nobody would feel a new rainfall climate. But a ten-year average is precise, and in four countries, Brazil, India, Kenya and Senegal, the change is measurable: Kenya −14 percent. And it does not follow the warming. So: temperature has clearly moved; rainfall much less. But when in the year does the warming happen?

**What to point at:**
- Mexico (+5.2σ);
- Germany (+2.9σ);
- Australia (the only hollow red dot);
- then Kenya and Senegal on the rainfall side.

### The main points

**Filled versus hollow dots.** A filled dot means the change is beyond 2σ. The only hollow temperature dot is Australia's. On the rainfall side every dot is hollow.

**Why Mexico beats Germany.** Germany warmed more in degrees (+1.85 vs +1.43 °C). But German years wobble a lot (σ = 0.64 °C, cold or mild winters), while Mexican years hardly wobble (σ = 0.27 °C). So the same warming stands out far more in Mexico: 1.43 / 0.27 = 5.2, against 1.85 / 0.64 = 2.9. *What matters for people is the change compared with what they are used to.*

**"0 of 12" and "measurable in 4" are not a contradiction.** They answer two different questions:

| Question | Compare the change with… | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Would people *feel* a new rainfall climate? | σ, the swing of **one** ordinary year | 0 of 12 beyond 2σ |
| Has the **average** rainfall really shifted? | the precision of a **10-year average** against a 30-year one: σ × √(1/10 + 1/30), about a third of σ | 4 of 12 beyond 2 of these units |

A ten-year average cancels most of the year-to-year wobble. That makes a small shift detectable even though no single year would feel unusual. Kenya: Δ = −14.2 %, σ = 17.3 %, so the precision unit is 17.3 × 0.365 = 6.3 %, and −14.2 / 6.3 = −2.2.

**"It does not follow the warming."** Across the twelve countries, the countries that warmed most do not have the biggest rainfall changes. If anything it is the reverse: the rank correlation between warming and the size of the rainfall change is −0.42. With 12 countries, anything within ±0.6 is indistinguishable from no relation, so we only say "it does not follow".

## Your question: why a "gap" for temperature but no reference period for rainfall?

There are two possible "gaps", and neither means missing data.

1. **The gap Δ (the arrow on slide 4).** This is the change itself: the distance between the old normal (zero by construction) and the 2016–2025 average. Rainfall has exactly the same gap, computed the same way. We only *draw* the method on one variable for time. The rainfall Δs are on slide 6 (right panel, "Change 2016–2025 vs 1961–1990 (%)").
2. **The blank columns on slide 4** (1991, 1996, 2010): years a hair below zero, so the bar is a tiny blue sliver.

**Rainfall does have a reference period: the same one, 1961–1990.** It was only implicit in the label "% of normal". It is now visible:
- slide 5 has a "reference 1961–1990" bar above *both* panels, and both titles say "vs 1961–1990";
- the footer of slide 4 says "rainfall: same steps, in % of its 1961–1990 average";
- your text says it out loud ("Rainfall goes through exactly the same steps, against the same reference, in percent of normal").

## Questions you are most likely to get on slides 4–6

**Why not compare 2025 alone with the past?** One year is mostly noise. France 2025 was +1.84 °C but 2021 only +0.87. A ten-year mean is stable.

**Why 2016–2025 and not 1991–2020 (the newer WMO normal)?** We want the most recent climate, and 1991–2020 overlaps the warming itself. The 10-year window is short enough to be "now" and long enough to average out single years.

**Does the choice of baseline change the result?** For temperature it shifts every anomaly by the same constant, so the colours shift but the trend and σ do not move. Δ would be smaller with a later baseline, since part of the warming would already be "normal". That is why we use a baseline before most of the warming.

**Is σ the same as the standard deviation of the data?** No. It is the standard deviation *around the trend line*. The plain standard deviation would include the warming and look bigger (for France it would mix the 1960s and the 2020s).

**Why is Australia below 2σ?** It warmed the least (+0.69 °C, tied with India), and its years swing a lot (σ = 0.41), because El Niño and La Niña push whole years up or down.

**Isn't 92 % a strange number to quote?** It is the share of the variance of France's monthly temperature that disappears when we subtract the monthly normals: 1 − 2.62 / 34.12 = 0.92. It is just a way to say "the seasons are almost everything you see".

**Why are some rainfall trends significant (Table 1) if no change exceeds 2σ?** A trend uses all 65 years and is much more precise than a decade-to-baseline comparison against σ. Kenya, Senegal and Spain have significant drying trends. They are real but small compared with one year's swing.
